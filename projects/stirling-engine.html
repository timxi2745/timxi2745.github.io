PHYSICS / THERMODYNAMICS / EXPERIMENTAL ANALYSIS

Stirling Engine Cart

Experimental investigation of Stirling engine efficiency through thermodynamic modeling, CAD-based mechanical analysis, and experimental testing.

Course Physics 4C
Team Tianwei Xi & Collin Wong
Test Distance 3.05 m
Trials 10

Overview

This project investigated the efficiency of a model Stirling Engine mounted to a cart. The objective was to experimentally determine the engine's efficiency and compare the measured result with the theoretical efficiency predicted by the Stirling cycle.

The engine converted thermal energy from an alcohol lamp into mechanical work, causing the cart to travel a measured distance of 3.05 meters. Both translational and rotational work were considered when determining the total mechanical output.

The experimental analysis incorporated measured travel time, cart mass, fuel consumption, and wheel dynamics.

Thermodynamic Model

An ideal Stirling Engine operates through alternating isothermal and isochoric processes. The theoretical efficiency was calculated using the temperature ratio between the cold and hot sides of the engine.

η = 1 − TC / TH

The experiment used room temperature for the cold-side temperature and the combustion temperature of denatured alcohol as the hot-side temperature.

Experimental Method

The Stirling Engine cart was tested over a fixed 3.05-meter track. The cart was allowed to reach operating speed before being placed on the track, and a stopwatch was used to measure the travel time.

The mass of the system was measured before and after each run to determine the amount of fuel consumed. Ten trials were performed.

Approximately 17–18 wheel revolutions occurred over the 3.05-meter track, indicating little to no wheel slip.

CAD-Based Mechanical Analysis

CAD software was used to determine the mass properties of the wheel because of its complex geometry. The calculated moment of inertia about the wheel's axis of rotation was then used to determine the rotational work.

0.02023 kg Wheel mass
9.8488 × 10⁻⁶ kg·m² Wheel moment of inertia
54.8 mm Wheel diameter

The rotational contribution to total work was calculated for all four wheels using the wheel moment of inertia and angular velocity.

Mechanical Work Analysis

Total mechanical output was determined by combining the translational work of the cart with the rotational work of its four wheels.

Wtotal = ½mv² + 4(½Iω²)

Translational velocity was determined from measured displacement and travel time. Angular velocity was then calculated from the wheel radius and cart velocity.

Across the ten trials, the average total mechanical work produced by the system was approximately 0.0433 J.

Results

The experimental efficiency was substantially lower than the theoretical value. The analysis identified heat losses, friction, fuel losses during startup, measurement uncertainty, and limitations of the idealized temperature assumptions as contributing factors.

Engineering Discussion

One major limitation was the assumption that the full combustion energy of the denatured alcohol entered the Stirling Engine as useful heat input. In practice, only a fraction of the combustion energy reaches the working gas, while additional energy is lost to the surroundings and through the mechanical components.

The analysis also identified friction within the engine, rolling resistance at the wheels, fuel consumed while the engine reached steady speed, stopwatch uncertainty, and variation in the actual path of the cart as sources of experimental error.

The results demonstrate the difference between an ideal thermodynamic model and the performance of a real mechanical system.
