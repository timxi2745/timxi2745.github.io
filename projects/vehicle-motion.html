MATLAB / DATA ANALYSIS / VEHICLE DYNAMICS

Vehicle Motion Data Analysis

MATLAB-based performance modeling and experimental analysis of a 2016 Subaru Forester using vehicle motion and fuel consumption data.

Software MATLAB
Team Tianwei X, Chris Y, Loki B, Jason W
Vehicle 2016 Subaru Forester
Data OBD Measurements

Overview

This team project developed a performance model for a 2016 Subaru Forester using experimentally collected vehicle data. The study analyzed position, velocity, acceleration, engine fuel rate, and RPM to model vehicle behavior under different driving conditions.

MATLAB was used to process the collected measurements, develop predictive models, and visualize vehicle performance. The models were then used to extrapolate vehicle behavior across three test courses and the Irwindale track.

Experimental Data Collection

Vehicle data was collected using a Veepeak Mini OBD reader and the Car Scanner application. The initial testing focused on acceleration from rest to 35 mph, which provided the primary dataset used to develop the performance models.

Additional testing was performed on a closed course at Mt. San Antonio College. Three trials were completed for each course under approximately the same conditions.

Course 1 0 → 15 mph, then maintain speed to 100 yd
Course 2 0 → 25 mph, then decelerate over 200 yd
Course 3 ¼ mile at approximately 15 mph average
Irwindale Performance extrapolation

MATLAB Data Processing

The collected measurements were organized and processed before being analyzed in MATLAB. Units were converted to SI units and MATLAB's built-in functions were used to visualize relationships between position, velocity, acceleration, fuel usage, and time.

The modeling approach divided each driving course into separate sections, allowing different portions of the vehicle's motion to be represented using appropriate kinematic relationships.

For example, constant acceleration was used to model the initial acceleration portions of the courses, while constant velocity was represented using a linear position relationship.

Vehicle Motion Modeling

Position, velocity, and acceleration were analyzed as functions of time. Constant-acceleration kinematic relationships were used to model the vehicle during acceleration, while constant-velocity sections were represented using linear relationships.

The Course 1 model predicted an initially parabolic distance-versus-time relationship during acceleration, followed by a constant slope once the vehicle reached approximately 15 mph.

The Course 2 model incorporated both acceleration and deceleration. Comparison with experimental data showed that the model captured several portions of the motion, while deviations occurred during the transition to a complete stop.

Fuel Consumption Analysis

Fuel usage was modeled as a function of time using an experimentally determined fuel consumption rate of approximately 0.00256 gallons per second.

The performance models relatively predicted the fuel behavior of Courses 1 and 3. The analysis also showed a relationship between acceleration and increasing fuel usage during the Irwindale extrapolation.

The Irwindale model predicted approximately 0.15 gallons of fuel consumption over 50 seconds.

RPM Analysis

RPM data presented an additional challenge during the experiment. The Car Scanner data export did not provide usable RPM measurements, so RPM values for the initial 0–35 mph dataset were approximated using the relationship between angular velocity, vehicle velocity, and tire radius.

Because RPM measurements were not collected for the three course trials, the predictive accuracy of the RPM model for those courses could not be evaluated.

The report also identified an important limitation of estimating engine RPM from wheel motion: engine RPM can differ from wheel RPM because the engine can operate independently of vehicle motion, particularly while the vehicle is idling.

Irwindale Performance Model

The MATLAB models were extrapolated to predict vehicle performance on the Irwindale track. The model predicted approximately 23 m/s as the vehicle's top speed and an acceleration of approximately 5 m/s² during the modeled acceleration section.

The model also predicted an increase in fuel usage as the vehicle accelerated, followed by a reduction in the rate of fuel consumption as the vehicle approached a stop.

Results & Limitations

Comparison between the experimental data and the performance models showed that the models captured several overall trends in vehicle motion. However, discrepancies were observed between the experimental results and previously published vehicle performance values.

Several limitations affected the analysis, including the relatively small dataset, limited variation in the acceleration tests, missing RPM measurements, and the short duration of the fuel-consumption measurements.

The project also demonstrated that average speed alone does not describe how speed changes throughout a trip, which created additional challenges when modeling Course 3.

Engineering Takeaways

This project strengthened my experience using MATLAB for engineering data processing, visualization, and performance modeling. It also provided hands-on experience connecting experimental vehicle data with fundamental mechanics and kinematic relationships.

A major takeaway was the importance of collecting sufficient and diverse experimental data when developing predictive engineering models. Comparing experimental measurements with theoretical models helped identify both the strengths and limitations of the modeling approach.

Project Outcome

The final performance models provided a practical way to examine and predict vehicle behavior across different driving conditions. The project demonstrated how experimental data, physics-based equations, and MATLAB visualization can be combined to investigate real-world vehicle performance.
